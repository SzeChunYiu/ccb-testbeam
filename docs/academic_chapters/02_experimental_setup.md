# §2 — Experimental Setup: The CCB Test-Beam and HRD Scintillator Range Telescope

The CCB test-beam experiment was conducted at the Cyclotron Centre Bronowice (CCB) in Kraków, Poland. A proton beam of 190 MeV kinetic energy impinged on a deuterated polyethylene (CD₂) target, producing a mixed field of scattered protons, deuterons, and nuclear fragments whose detection and identification form the central physics programme of this work. The primary motivation is the characterisation of the High-Rate Detector (HRD) scintillator range stacks, which constitute the charged-particle detection system of the HIBEAM/NNBAR experiment at the European Spallation Source (ESS) [1]. These detectors must deliver same-particle timing resolution below one nanosecond while operating in a high-rate environment where pile-up — the temporal overlap of signals from multiple beam particles within a single acquisition window — is a limiting systematic.

## Beamline and detector geometry

The experimental apparatus comprises four subsystems arranged along the beam axis: a trigger scintillator system, a Time Projection Chamber (TPC) for charged-particle tracking, and two HRD scintillator range telescopes designated the A-stack and B-stack. The A-stack sits at approximately +71.5° relative to the incident beam direction, subtending the recoil arm of proton-deuteron scattering kinematics. The B-stack occupies the downstream arm at approximately −38°. Both stacks are positioned roughly 100 cm from the CD₂ target. The B-stack, consisting of eight scintillator staves numbered B0 through B14, is the primary analysis system; of these, the even-numbered staves B2, B4, B6, and B8 are instrumented for waveform readout. The A-stack, with staves A1 and A3 instrumented, provides a decoupled cross-check measurement that is not used in the quantitative analysis but serves as a consistency test of the trigger logic and scattering kinematics.

## Scintillator stave design and readout

Each instrumented stave is a plastic scintillator bar optically coupled to a wavelength-shifting (WLS) optical fibre. The WLS fibre absorbs the primary scintillation light — emitted in the ultraviolet-to-blue region by the plastic scintillator — and re-emits it at a longer wavelength that matches the spectral sensitivity of silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs). The fibre guides the wavelength-shifted light to a single SiPM at one end of the stave, yielding a one-ended readout configuration. The characteristic propagation velocity of light in the WLS fibre is approximately 17 cm ns⁻¹. When a charged particle traverses the scintillator, the resulting optical pulse is converted to an electrical signal by the SiPM and digitised by a flash analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) operating at 100 megasamples per second, corresponding to a sampling interval of 10 ns. Each recorded waveform spans 18 samples, defining a 180 ns acquisition window.

The one-ended readout introduces a position-dependent timing offset: light generated near the instrumented end of the stave arrives promptly, whereas light produced at the distal end experiences both attenuation in the WLS fibre and a propagation delay of approximately 5.9 ns for a 100 cm stave at 17 cm ns⁻¹. This position dependence is the dominant contribution to the single-stave timing resolution and motivates the projected factor-of-√2 improvement for a future two-ended readout, in which both ends of each WLS fibre are instrumented [2]. The digitised waveform captures the full scintillation pulse shape. The pulse rise time, typically 20–30 ns (2–3 samples), is determined by the convolution of the scintillator decay constant and the SiPM response function. The fall time is dominated by the WLS fibre decay time and SiPM recovery. The pulse amplitude, extracted as the maximum ADC value above a per-waveform baseline, serves as the primary energy estimator. The pulse arrival time is extracted using either a constant-fraction discriminator (CFD) at 20% of peak amplitude or an optimal filter (OF) matched to an average pulse template [3].

## Trigger configurations and data samples

The trigger system defines two data-taking configurations that correspond to two physically distinct event samples. Sample I, spanning runs 31–57 with run 43 excluded owing to data quality issues, employs a coincidence trigger: the trigger scintillators placed in front of both the A-stack and the B-stack must fire in temporal coincidence. Sample II, spanning runs 58–65, employs a single-arm trigger: only the B-stack trigger scintillator is required to fire, regardless of the state of the A-stack trigger. Runs 31–42 and run 64 are reserved as calibration runs for timing-correction studies. Runs 38, 45, 49, 52, and 57 are absent from the A-stack data, reflecting the lower priority of the A-stack readout during data taking.

The trigger configuration enriches the samples in distinct particle populations, an effect confirmed by the GEANT4 Monte Carlo simulation. The coincidence requirement of Sample I selects events in which at least one charged particle scatters into both the A and B arms simultaneously. Kinematically, this favours quasi-elastic proton-deuteron scattering (p + d → p + d), where the two-body final state produces a correlated proton-deuteron pair. Sample II, with its single-arm trigger, accepts a broader mixture dominated by protons from deuteron breakup and inelastic reactions. The simulation predicts that 73.5% of charged particles entering the B-stack in Sample I are deuterons, compared to 48.4% in Sample II.

## Monte Carlo simulation and trigger mimicry

The GEANT4 simulation uses the HIBEAM/NNBAR `hibeam_g4` framework with the Krakow beamline geometry [4]. A 190 MeV proton beam is directed onto a CD₂ target, and the resulting interactions are propagated through the full detector geometry, including the trigger scintillators, TPC, and both HRD stacks. The simulation records per-hit truth information in a `hibeam` ROOT tree: particle identity via the Particle Data Group (PDG) Monte Carlo numbering scheme [5], energy deposition in MeV, hit time in nanoseconds, hit position in millimetres, and particle momentum at each scintillator hit.

The hardware trigger conditions are mimicked in the simulation by applying geometric and timing cuts at truth level. For the Sample II (single-B) condition, a charged particle must enter the first layer of the B-stack (Sci_bar_LayerID1 = 1, LayerID = 0). For the Sample I (coincidence) condition, charged particles must enter the first layer of both the A-stack (Sci_bar_LayerID1 = 2, LayerID = 0) and the B-stack within a 15 ns coincidence window: |t_A − t_B| < 15 ns. This definition ensures that the simulated Sample I is a strict subset of the simulated Sample II, exactly mirroring the hardware trigger logic.

## Data acquisition and processing

The raw dataset comprises 110 ROOT files totalling approximately 810 MB for the compressed B-stack data, organised by run number and detector stack (HRDA for the A-stack, HRDB for the B-stack). The analysis pipeline reduces these raw waveform files to a selected-pulse table: per-waveform baseline subtraction is performed using the pre-trigger samples; the pulse amplitude is extracted as the maximum baseline-subtracted ADC value within the 180 ns window; and a selection cut of amplitude A > 1000 ADC is applied to reject electronic noise and low-energy background. This yields 640,737 selected B-stack pulses when using the median baseline estimator. Each selected pulse is recorded with its run number, event number, stave identifier, baseline ADC, amplitude ADC, peak sample index, and integrated pulse area in ADC-samples.

The conversion from ADC units to physical energy deposition relies on a digitizer gain calibration obtained by matching the Monte Carlo energy deposition spectrum to the data amplitude spectrum. Using the Sample II (proton-dominated) first-layer median as a single calibration point yields a preliminary scale factor of 245.6 ± 73.7 ADC MeV⁻¹, corresponding to a fractional systematic uncertainty of ±30%. This large uncertainty arises from three sources: the reliance on a single calibration point rather than a multi-stave energy scan, approximations in the digitizer model (which omits SiPM saturation and Birks quenching at high energy deposition), and the absence of forced-trigger pedestal data that would anchor the baseline subtraction.

## Range telescope principle and particle identification

The HRD stacks operate as range telescopes: the depth to which a charged particle penetrates before stopping, together with the pattern of energy deposition across successive staves, encodes the particle's identity. As a charged particle traverses the scintillator staves, it loses energy according to the Bethe-Bloch stopping power formula [6], with the mean energy loss per unit path length increasing as the particle slows — the characteristic Bragg peak behaviour. Heavier particles have shorter range than lighter particles at the same incident kinetic energy. At 190 MeV, deuterons (rest mass 1875.6 MeV c⁻²) have a range in plastic scintillator of approximately 5–8 cm, causing them to stop predominantly in staves B2 or B4. Protons (rest mass 938.3 MeV c⁻²) at the same energy have a range exceeding 20 cm and typically penetrate to staves B6 or B8. This range difference produces the observed depth profile: Sample I, being deuteron-enriched, concentrates 95.7% of its pulses in B2, while Sample II distributes its pulses more evenly across all four staves, with only 70.5% in B2.

The traditional ΔE-E method exploits the correlation between the energy deposited in the first traversed stave (ΔE) and the total energy deposited or the residual range (E) to separate particle species. Originally developed for silicon detector telescopes in low-energy nuclear physics [7] and subsequently adapted to scintillator-based range stacks [8], the method produces distinct loci in the ΔE-E plane for different particle species when the stopping powers differ sufficiently.

## Systematic considerations

Several detector effects complicate the simple range-telescope picture and must be accounted for in the analysis. First, the one-ended WLS readout produces position-dependent light collection: particles stopping near the instrumented end deposit more collected light than identical particles stopping at the distal end, introducing a position-dependent amplitude scale that broadens the ΔE-E loci. Second, the B2 stave saturates for large energy depositions: 41.7% of Sample I B2 pulses and 6.1% of Sample II B2 pulses exceed the ADC saturation ceiling of approximately 7000 ADC, truncating the energy measurement for the most heavily ionising particles. The Monte Carlo simulation does not currently model this saturation. Third, the pile-up of multiple beam particles within the 180 ns acquisition window distorts both the amplitude and timing of individual pulses; this is the subject of the dedicated pile-up analysis in §5. Fourth, the Birks quenching effect [9] — the saturation of scintillation light yield per unit energy deposition at high ionisation density — reduces the light output for low-energy, highly ionising particles (stopping deuterons near the Bragg peak) relative to minimum-ionising particles, requiring an empirical calibration.

# Chapter 1: Introduction and Executive Summary

> **Abstract-style overview of the CCB test-beam analysis programme. Target: 2,500 words. Every claim traceable to specific studies and data.**

---

The CCB test-beam experiment was conducted at the Cyclotron Centre Bronowice in Krakow, Poland, as part of the detector development programme for the HIBEAM/NNBAR experiment at the European Spallation Source (ESS). A proton beam of 190 MeV kinetic energy impinged on a deuterated polyethylene (CD2) target, producing a mixed field of scattered protons, deuterons, and nuclear fragments. Two High-Rate Detector (HRD) scintillator range telescopes — the A-stack and B-stack — measured the energy deposition and arrival time of charged particles traversing successive scintillator staves, with the B-stack (staves B2, B4, B6, B8) serving as the primary analysis system. Each stave recorded 18-sample ADC waveforms at 100 megasamples per second, yielding a 180 ns acquisition window per event. The raw dataset comprises 110 ROOT files totalling approximately 810 MB for the compressed B-stack, from which 640,737 selected pulse records were extracted by applying a baseline-subtracted amplitude threshold of A > 1000 ADC. This selection anchor is reproduced with exact fidelity (zero-delta) against the original analysis note, verified by SHA256 checksums in Study S00.

The analysis programme addresses three interconnected physics goals. First, the same-particle timing resolution of the HRD staves is characterised by constructing inter-stave time residuals from constant-fraction discriminator (CFD) arrival times and correcting for amplitude-dependent timewalk via the analytic form f(A) = A0 + B/A, where A is the pulse amplitude, A0 is the asymptotic CFD offset, and B/A captures the leading-order residual from non-exponential pulse shape and finite SiPM bandwidth. The best single-stave resolution is achieved by B6 (sigma_68 = 0.68-0.75 ns), with the combined B4+B6+B8 weighted-average time reaching sigma_68 = 0.54-0.56 ns. The B2 stave is excluded from precision timing due to topology-driven covariance (covariance approximately 1042 ns^2 for B2-X pairs versus approximately 16 ns^2 for downstream pairs), a physics effect arising from stopping deuterons whose saturating Bragg-peak energy depositions distort the CFD time. The analytic timewalk correction matches or exceeds the performance of machine-learning alternatives (histogram gradient boosting, multi-layer perceptron, convolutional neural network) under rigorous leave-one-run-out cross-validation, with apparent ML improvements narrowing or disappearing when the models are required to generalise to unseen runs. This finding — that traditional physics-anchored methods remain competitive with deep learning when evaluated under proper leakage controls — is a recurring theme across the analysis programme.

Second, the pile-up tolerance of the detector is quantified through a combination of direct waveform live-time measurement, Poisson rate modelling, and Monte Carlo validation. The effective waveform live-time, defined as the time for the pulse template to fall to 10% of its peak amplitude, is measured to be tau_eff = 124.79 ns (bootstrap 68% confidence interval: [123.33, 126.36] ns). This is 39% larger than the 90 ns assumed in the original analysis note, which used only the scintillator decay time and neglected wavelength-shifting fibre dispersion and SiPM recovery. The corrected maximum tolerable beam rate is R_max = 3.05 MHz, derived independently from occupancy-limit and recovery-failure-limit definitions that agree to 0.2%. Monte Carlo pile-up simulation (Study MV5) confirms R_max(MC) = 3.044 MHz. Two-pulse decomposition — the recovery of individual pulse amplitudes and times from overlapping waveforms — is evaluated for both constrained template fitting and machine-learning regression. The template fit achieves a failure rate of 0.168 with time RMS of 13.30 ns; the ML approach achieves better RMS (9.28-10.67 ns) but a higher failure rate (0.295). The conventional template fit is recommended for production analysis pending a truth-labelled Monte Carlo overlay study that would characterise ML failure modes.

Third, the particle identification capability of the HRD range telescope is assessed through the deltaE-E method, where the energy deposited in B2 (deltaE analogue) is correlated with the energy deposited in B4 (E analogue). The HRD stacks function as range telescopes: heavier particles (deuterons, rest mass 1875.6 MeV/c^2) have shorter range than lighter particles (protons, rest mass 938.3 MeV/c^2) at the same incident kinetic energy, causing deuterons to stop predominantly in B2-B4 while protons penetrate to B6-B8. The GEANT4 Monte Carlo simulation, using the HIBEAM/NNBAR hibeam_g4 framework with the Krakow beamline geometry, provides truth-level particle identity for every scintillator hit. The trigger configuration defines two data-taking modes: Sample I (runs 31-57, coincidence trigger requiring both A-stack and B-stack trigger scintillators to fire) and Sample II (runs 58-65, single-B trigger). The Monte Carlo confirms that Sample I is deuteron-enriched: 73.5% of charged particles entering the B-stack in Sample I are deuterons, compared to 48.4% in Sample II — a factor of 1.52 enrichment from the coincidence trigger requirement. This enrichment produces a large-pulse excess in the first B layer (B2) that is observed in both Monte Carlo (fraction of pulses above 15 MeV EDep: 0.730 for Sample I versus 0.481 for Sample II) and data (fraction above 6000 ADC: 0.588 for Sample I versus 0.117 for Sample II). The data excess is larger than the Monte Carlo excess (0.471 versus 0.249) due to B2 saturation at high amplitude (41.7% of Sample I B2 pulses exceed the 7000 ADC saturation ceiling), an effect not currently modelled in the digitizer.

The Monte Carlo validation programme (MV0-MV6) provides a systematic truth-bridged assessment of every physics claim. The MV0 digitizer converts GEANT4 truth-level energy depositions into synthetic 18-sample ADC waveforms by modelling scintillator time response (BC-408 plastic scintillator: tau_rise = 2.0 ns, tau_decay = 35.0 ns), wavelength-shifting fibre transport (Gaussian time dispersion sigma_transport = 0.5 ns), 100 MHz sampling, Gaussian electronic noise (sigma_noise = 50 ADC), and an optional saturation ceiling at 7000 ADC. This enables the identical analysis code to run on both data and digitised Monte Carlo with truth labels attached. The digitizer gain calibration, obtained by matching the Sample II proton-dominated first-layer median, yields 245.6 plus or minus 73.7 ADC/MeV (plus or minus 30% systematic), with the uncertainty arising from single-point calibration, missing forced-trigger pedestal data, and digitizer model approximations.

The MC validation results are mixed. Raw timing passes (sigma_68: MC 1.744 plus or minus 0.007 ns versus data 1.85 ns, pull = 1.05 sigma). Timewalk-corrected timing shows tension (sigma_68: MC 1.770 ns versus data 1.50 ns, pull = 2.68 sigma), traced to an unphysical negative B coefficient in the digitizer CFD model that uses B/sqrt(ADC) instead of the correct B/amplitude parametrisation. The particle ID ceiling at truth level reaches AUC = 0.986 (Study MV1), establishing the achievable upper bound for any data-driven PID method. The anomaly class discovered by unsupervised waveform clustering — 0.32% of tracks with early-peaking (sample 1-2 instead of sample 5) and near-zero integrated area — is identified by Monte Carlo truth as carbon-12 nuclear recoils (55% of anomalies) from proton scattering off carbon nuclei in the CD2 target, depositing all energy in the first approximately 25 micrometres of scintillator. The stopping-depth profile shows a structural Monte Carlo failure (Study MV3, GAP-01): the simulation overestimates B8 penetration by a factor of ten relative to data, with chi^2/ndf = 68,269, caused by missing upstream material budget (absorbers, support structures, trigger scintillators, beam window, inter-stave dead material, estimated at 8-10 g/cm^2) in the GEANT4 geometry.

The most important methodological finding of this analysis programme is that rigorous leakage controls are essential for evaluating machine learning in physics. Across the approximately 230 completed data-driven studies, multiple apparent ML wins were subsequently corrected when subjected to run-family shuffle, event-block shuffle, or leave-one-run-out cross-validation. The representation-superiority claim for autoencoder-based pulse embeddings was traced to a run-family leakage artefact. The deep-network timing advantage over analytic timewalk was reduced to a tie or loss under proper cross-validation. The near-perfect PID AUC of approximately 1.0 from curvature-based classifiers was identified as a self-referential label where the label is a deterministic function of the input waveform. These corrections are not failures — they are findings that establish the methodological standard for ML evaluation in detector physics. Machine learning does win in specific domains where the missing information is genuinely encoded in waveform shape: saturation recovery (3-7 times improvement over traditional methods) and duplicate-readout amplitude closure (residual_68 = 0.003 versus 0.12 for the conventional method). In both cases, the truth label is independent of the input waveform, satisfying the fundamental requirement for supervised learning.

The analysis does not find that machine learning should fully replace traditional methods. Physics-anchored approaches — analytic timewalk, Poisson pile-up rate estimation, Birks-law energy calibration — remain superior or tied in their respective domains, with the additional advantages of transparency, physical interpretability, and verified cross-run stability. The lesson is not that ML is useless in detector physics, but that it must be evaluated under the same rigorous controls as any other analysis method, with explicit attention to label independence, out-of-sample generalisation, and comparison against a strong traditional baseline rather than a degraded one.

The systematic uncertainty budget is dominated by the digitizer gain calibration (plus or minus 30% from MV0), followed by stopping-depth model uncertainty (5% from MV3 geometry incompleteness), timing validation tension (3% from the MV4 timewalk discrepancy), and the C12 anomaly contribution (0.1%, negligible for all physics quantities). The one-ended wavelength-shifting fibre readout introduces a position-dependent timing offset that is the dominant irreducible contribution to the single-stave resolution. A two-ended readout, where both ends of each fibre are instrumented, is projected to improve timing by a factor of approximately sqrt(2), reducing the single-stave resolution to sigma_68 approximately 0.48-0.53 ns, though this projection assumes uncorrelated end measurements and remains to be validated with dedicated split-readout data.

The operational implications for the HIBEAM/NNBAR experiment at the ESS are twofold. First, the validated pile-up tolerance of R_max approximately 3.05 MHz translates to a per-stave rate limit of approximately 30 kHz during ESS beam-on periods (14 Hz pulse rate, 2.86 ms pulse width, 4.0% duty factor), which is achievable with the planned beam intensity provided that passive shielding reduces the low-energy neutron background. Second, the B2 covariance problem means that the first stave functions primarily as a trigger and particle-identification element, with precision timing reserved for the deeper staves (B4, B6, B8) where the particle population is cleaner. This is a deliberate detector design choice, not a deficiency.

The repository `SzeChunYiu/ccb-testbeam` contains the complete analysis codebase, all study reports, the GEANT4 simulation configuration, and the MC validation pipeline. The selected-pulse table, digitizer configuration, and all intermediate data products are version-controlled with SHA256 checksums. The analysis is research-in-progress, preliminary, and not yet peer-reviewed. The findings documented here represent the state of the analysis as of July 2026, with open questions and planned studies catalogued in the Study Gaps analysis.

## Open Questions

1. **Stopping-depth Monte Carlo failure (GAP-01, blocking):** The GEANT4 geometry is missing 8-10 g/cm^2 of upstream material. Until fixed, quantitative MC-based acceptance corrections are unreliable.

2. **Timewalk Monte Carlo tension (GAP-02, high):** The digitizer CFD model needs a code-level fix from B/sqrt(ADC) to B/amplitude to resolve the +2.68 sigma discrepancy.

3. **Digitizer gain uncertainty (GAP-03, high):** The plus or minus 30% systematic requires forced-trigger pedestal data and a multi-stave calibration scan to reduce to plus or minus 10-15%.

4. **Two-pulse ML failure rate (GAP-04, medium):** A truth-labelled Monte Carlo overlay study is needed to characterise and reduce the 0.295 ML failure rate below the 0.168 template ceiling.

5. **Two-ended timing projection (GAP-05, medium):** The sqrt(2) improvement factor for two-ended readout is unvalidated and requires dedicated split-readout measurements.

6. **Absolute time-of-flight scale (GAP-08, low):** No independent TOF reference (TPC, trigger scintillator cross-check) has been calibrated.
